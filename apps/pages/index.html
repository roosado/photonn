An optical neural network, running live

This neural network is made of light

A handwritten digit is written into a beam of light. The beam crosses five plates of glass, each with a pattern etched into its surface, and the answer is simply where the light lands. Nothing in that sentence is electronic: the etched patterns hold everything an ordinary neural network would keep as numbers in memory. It runs below, in your browser. The rest of this site asks what a machine like this can and cannot do, and which of the flaws in a real one takes the answer away first.

What you just watched

Every panel is real light, not an illustration

Light is a wave, and a wave gives you two things to adjust. How tall it is, its amplitude, is what makes light bright or dim. Where it sits in its up-and-down cycle, its phase, is what decides whether two waves meeting each other reinforce or cancel. Your eye sees the first and is completely blind to the second. This machine runs on both: hold one wave back by half a wavelength and two beams of light add up to darkness.

So the entrance field is your digit written into the brightness and the phase of the beam. The five small frames are the brightness arriving at each plate. Each plate is a phase mask, a surface that holds the light back by a different amount at every point across it, and that is the name this site uses from here on. Watch the digit dissolve into a fine scramble that means nothing to the eye and everything to the detectors. The detector plane is the brightness at the far end with the ten class regions drawn on it, and the answer is whichever box collects the most light: no electronic layer, no learned classifier on top, just ten sums.

The 3D view is the same run drawn as a physical object: seven parallel planes along the path of the beam, each carrying the light actually computed on it, with the light between them drawn as haze. The haze is not shading. It is the real light at those in-between depths, worked out the same way as everything else, and the physics page shows why that is exact rather than an interpolation → Toggle Mask phase to swap the arriving light for the etched surface that acts on it.

There is no electronic network anywhere in this. Every step the light takes is linear: double the input and the output doubles, add two inputs together and you get the sum of what each would have produced alone. Exactly one step in the machine breaks that rule, and it is the detector, which measures brightness. Brightness is the square of the wave’s height (@@MATH_intensity@@), and squaring is not linear. That is the whole computation, and it is also the ceiling on what the computation can do.

The motivation, stated honestly

Why build a computer out of light?

Because the expensive part of the arithmetic above is free. A neural network is mostly one operation repeated billions of times: multiply two numbers, add the result to a running total. In a processor that operation is already cheap. At 45 nm a 32-bit floating-point add costs about 0.9 pJ, and a simpler fixed-point one roughly a ninth of that. What costs is fetching the two numbers to feed it. Those same 32 bits read out of memory on the chip cost 5 pJ, and out of the main memory alongside it 640 pJ, roughly a thousand times the operation they feed.1

The stack above never fetches anything. Every point of the beam influences every detector because that is what a wave does over 3 mm of air. Connecting everything to everything is the expensive part of a neural network layer, and here it is performed by the light spreading out on its own, with nothing charged, nothing switched, and no stored number read from anywhere. The trained values are never fetched because the trained values are the glass. And the computation finishes in the time light needs to cross 18 mm, which is 60 picoseconds, set by the speed of light and nothing else.

How little light does it need? The error budget measures exactly that. Accuracy holds flat from 1 mW all the way down to 1 pW over a 1 ms exposure, which is about one femtojoule of light per classification, and only below that does it fall off a cliff. Starve it further and watch the answer come apart, photon by photon →

That femtojoule is the energy in the light, not the energy to run the machine. The laser, the modulator that writes the digit into the beam, the ten detectors and their converters all cost more, and this project models none of them. At the design’s nominal operating point, 1 mW for 1 ms, a classification costs a microjoule, which a GPU beats comfortably. The claim the evidence supports is the narrow one: the part the optics does is nearly free, and everything around it is the engineering problem. That is roughly where the field itself sits.4

So the useful question is not whether light replaces a processor, but where free connections and an answer in 60 ps are worth the hardware around them. The candidates share a shape: the signal is already light, so nobody pays extra to put it there. Classify at a camera’s focal plane, before a pixel is digitised, and the optics does the first layer for nothing.5 Sort cells in a microscope faster than the readout can report them. Sit in front of a radar or radio receiver, where the digitiser rather than the arithmetic is the bottleneck.6 Go to orbit, where a plate of glass holds no charge for a cosmic ray to upset, which is part of why silicon photonics is being qualified for space at all.7 Every one of those still needs the hardware built to a precision nobody has quoted, which is what the rest of this site measures.

The stack above is the version you can watch: the whole trained operator is drawn on the glass, and the answer is a place on a screen.2 Most hardware actually being built is not free-space at all. It guides light along channels etched into silicon and mixes it in small steps,3 and there are further schemes again. What they share is the part that matters here, a linear transformation performed by light itself, so what the plates demonstrate carries over. This project builds the chip too, and they turn out to be the same machine →

What the trained surfaces are doing

How a phase mask computes

Two operations alternate, and both are linear. Crossing the 3 mm gap spreads every point of the beam outward into a small disc that overlaps its neighbours, by the same amount everywhere on the plate. That blending is what carries information sideways across the beam, and it is the step mathematicians call a convolution. Passing through a plate does the opposite. It touches each point on its own, holding it back by the depth etched at that spot and leaving its neighbours alone, which amounts to multiplying each point by @@MATH_maskphase@@, one delay per pixel. Those delays are the only thing training ever adjusts. Alternating the two, spread then delay, spread then delay, is exactly what a hologram does. This stack is five holograms that training wrote.

What the plates learn to do with that is steer light and bring it to a focus. Two waves that arrive in step add up and get brighter; two that arrive out of step cancel and go dark. The plates set the delays so that after all the spreading, an input of class c arrives in step over detector region c and out of step everywhere else. Early plates behave more like feature detectors, shuffling light around the whole beam; later ones behave more like lenses, gathering the light that identifies a class onto the right patch. About 60% of the light that enters ends up inside a detector box.

The other 40% is not waste waiting to be collected. Widening the boxes to catch it was measured against 48 different detector layouts, and none of them beat the one this network uses. Boxes wide enough to gather most of the light are wide enough to overlap, and then each one starts counting light that belongs to its neighbour. The light outside the boxes is not information going spare, it is the light the plates could not find a use for.

That steering picture is a story, not a proof, and it is worth saying so plainly. What is rigorously true is that the whole stack adds up to a single linear operation, and the plates are one way of building that operation out of parts you could actually manufacture. Choosing the task

Why a digit classifier, and only a digit classifier

The task is MNIST, a collection of 70,000 handwritten digits that machine-learning work has used as a first test for decades. It is the smallest honest version of the job: a real problem with a real error rate, it fits across the beam without contrivance, and it is easy enough that the optics stays the interesting part. The moment a task needs serious electronics bolted on the end to work at all, the optical network stops being the thing under study.

So the same ten digits are reused at every stage: the stack of plates, the silicon chip, and every one of the error sweeps that follow. One task, scored the same way on the same fixed set of 2,000 images the models never train on, is what makes those results comparable to each other, which is worth more here than a higher score on a harder task. The machine-learning content is deliberately minimal and stays that way.

Read the failures, not just the wins

It is wrong about one digit in four

The network reads 0.799 of the test digits correctly, so the gallery deliberately includes ones it gets wrong. Hiding them would misrepresent the model. Watch how the light is divided when it fails: a confident answer puts about 25–30% of the light in the winning box, a wrong one usually far less.

Drawings are harder still. Whatever we do, your handwriting will not look quite like the handwriting the model was trained on, so expect more errors there. To keep the comparison fair rather than flattering, a drawing is cleaned up the same way the MNIST images themselves were built: cropped to the ink, scaled so its longer side is 20 px, and centred by its balance point. That way a digit drawn too large or off to one side cannot be mistaken for a failure of the optics.

What your browser computes is not a simplified stand-in for the trained model. On the test digits it reproduces the predictions of the original PyTorch model exactly, with the ten scores agreeing to better than 10−3. Its delays are stored at 8 bits of precision, 256 possible settings per pixel, which is what a real device offers anyway, and that costs nothing measurable: 0.7995 against 0.7990 across 2,000 digits.

The mistakes are not spread evenly, and the pattern below is worth a look before you go hunting for one in the gallery. 1 is almost never missed, at 240 of 242. The hard class is 5, which is right only 109 times and is called a 3 on 41 of the rest. 8 goes the same way, landing on 3 thirty-seven times, and 9 and 4 trade places in both directions. Those are shape confusions rather than random ones: light routed by a surface cannot easily separate digits that share a closed loop in the same place.

Where this goes

What it would take to actually build one

Everything above ran in simulation, and simulation is where optical neural networks look easy. The masks are exact. The plates sit at exactly 3 mm. Every pixel takes exactly the phase it was trained to take, and its neighbour’s phase does not leak into it.

A real one has none of that, and it goes wrong in two distinct ways. Things wrong inside a part: etch depths vary, the device that writes the delays can only set them so finely, its field spills sideways so each pixel smears into the next, the laser drifts off its colour, and light is lost at every surface. And things wrong about where the parts sit: a plate mounted a micron off centre, a gap set to 3.1 mm rather than 3.0. The first kind is fixed the moment the part is made; the second is decided at assembly and can sometimes be calibrated back out. Neither kind is a bug to be fixed. They are the specification.

How much of each the network survives is a number, not an opinion, and getting it is what the rest of this project is. The trained delays are handed to a second, independent model of the machine as it would really be built, which breaks the network on purpose: ten imperfections, one at a time, then several together. One of them already fails against hardware you can buy today, and it is not the one you would guess →

The physics

The same diffraction calculation as the Python original, rewritten for the browser with no libraries and checked against it to better than 10−6. Six gaps of air and five plates per answer, in 532 nm green light on a 128×128 grid.

The parameters

Exported straight from the trained PyTorch model: 81,920 delays, stored at the 8 bits of precision a real device offers. Nothing is retrained or tuned to make the browser version look better.

Privacy

Everything happens on your machine. Nothing you draw is uploaded, stored, or sent anywhere. The page makes no network requests at all.
