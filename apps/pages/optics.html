How much better could the optics be?

The 5-mask network scores 0.799 and cannot be pushed further by training. After 40 passes through 60,000 images it still cannot pull ahead even on the images it trained on. So the remaining levers are physical: how far apart the masks sit, and how many there are. This page is the running record of measuring them. The numbers here move as more arrangements finish.

What scaling actually looks like

Eleven times the masks, ten points of accuracy

The network on the front page uses five masks. The obvious question is what happens with more, and the honest way to ask it is to hold everything else fixed: same total amount of light spreading, same grid, same task, only the number of plates changing. That sweep has been run from 2 masks up to 80.

It never turns over. More masks keep helping, all the way to the deepest configuration tested. But look at the shape of the help.

Live. Every point is a training run, read from the sweep’s own output rather than typed into the figure. Note the two colours: the cheap ranking runs and the two full-budget runs are not comparable to each other, and only the orange pair is comparable to the numbers quoted on the rest of this site.

Take the two full-budget runs, which are the only ones measured the same way as everything else here. Going from 5 masks to 56, an eleven-fold increase in the amount of hardware, moves accuracy from 0.7990 to 0.9040. Ten and a half points.

Put that next to what it costs. Eleven times the plates, and because the total spreading budget is fixed they have to be packed eleven times closer, at 0.53 mm apart instead of 3 mm. The stack grows from 18 mm to 30 mm. And, from the previous page, the fabrication tolerance tightens by 2× on every delay, needs one more bit of control per pixel, and allows 4.7× less lost light per surface, while the one error that already fails does not move at all.

That is a bad exchange rate, and it gets worse. The ranking curve is flattening hard: the last doubling of depth, 40 masks to 80, buys under half a point. There is no plausible number of masks that turns this into a competitive classifier. Something other than more of the same is required, and the rest of this page is about what.

See both machines run

The 5-mask and 56-mask networks, same digit

Since more masks keep paying, the deepest arrangement worth the computer time was trained properly rather than merely ranked: 56 masks at 0.53 mm gaps, the full 60,000 images, then scored once against the same held-back test images the 5-mask model is quoted from. It reaches 0.9040 against 0.7990. Both machines run here, live, on whichever digit you pick or one you draw, using the same physics as the Python original and checked against PyTorch to better than 10−3.

The gallery is the one the front page uses, deliberately stocked with six digits the 5-mask network gets wrong. The 56-mask network gets three of those right and breaks none of the ten the 5-mask one already had. The three it still misses, it misses in the same way, calling them the same wrong digit as the 5-mask model does, which hints that those images are hard for optics in general rather than for this particular set of masks.

Both machines are fed from one input, so the two columns differ in their optics and nothing else. Watch the detector plane rather than the answer. The 5-mask network spreads light across the whole plane and picks a weak winner out of it, while the deep stack drops the light inside the boxes. That is the difference between about 60% and 79% of the incoming photons reaching a detector at all, and it is the same steering that the accuracy gain comes from.

Two trained models come to 0.7 MB of delays on one page. The deep one carries eleven times as many numbers, 918k against 82k, for under six times the download, because its delays are stored more coarsely: 4 bits each, 16 possible settings, against 8 bits and 256. Neither model is flattered by that. The error budget measures this design as holding its accuracy all the way down to 3 bits, and on the same 2,000 digits the stored-down models score 0.9015 and 0.7995 against the full-precision 0.9040 and 0.7990, five thousandths either way, well inside the run-to-run noise of a test set that size. What runs in your browser is therefore the machine the study measured, not a lighter stand-in.

One pass through the optics costs roughly 11 ms and 100 ms respectively, since the deep machine is 56 diffraction steps of arithmetic running in a browser tab, and both are far more than the 17 ms an animation frame allows. So the board waits for you to pause rather than classifying mid-stroke, and it decides that by timing itself rather than by counting masks, so a board carrying only cheap models still follows the pen. The obvious alternative, keeping the fast column live and letting the deep one lag, was rejected on purpose: it would show two different digits side by side and invite exactly the wrong comparison.

What 56 masks actually looks like

The detector plane says the deep network is better. It does not say what the thing is. Here it is as a physical machine: the entrance, the phase plates and the detectors strung along the path of the beam, each carrying the light actually computed on it. Drag to orbit.

Two things about this figure are honest rather than convenient. It draws only 6 of the 56 masks, spread through the stack and each labelled with its real position, because fifty-six plates at 0.53 mm spacing are fifty-six near-identical pictures. And it does not follow your pen: redrawing means a full pass through 57 gaps, so it waits for you to press Refresh rather than making the whole page stutter for a picture nobody studies mid-stroke.

The proportions are the point. The 5-mask design is 18 mm of optics across an opening of 1.02 mm; this one is 30 mm across the same opening, a 29:1 needle that has to be squashed almost tenfold just to fit on a screen. Past about forty plates at this spacing, calling it a stack of separate masks is already a stretch. It is closer to a solid block of glass, which is a different thing to manufacture and one the error budget → does not model yet.

Three readings. The constraint that binds does not move. Crosstalk fails at the same 0.25 px, and a real device’s ~1 px of bleed destroys either design. Depth neither helps nor hurts the one thing that already made this unbuildable.

The two that loosened both come from catching more light. The deep stack lands 79.1% of incoming photons inside the detector boxes against about 60%, by the same steering the accuracy gain comes from. More light means more tolerance for dimming and for drift. It got sturdier exactly where it already had nine orders of magnitude in hand.

Lost light points in opposite directions depending on how you count. Totalled over the machine the 56-mask network tolerates more, 12 dB against 5. But that budget is divided among eleven times as many surfaces, so the per-plate requirement tightens to 0.214 dB, which sits at the optimistic end of a realistic 0.2–1 dB range. Datasheets quote per surface, so the per-surface number is the one that governs.

Finding · depth adds a constraint without relieving one

Ten and a half points of accuracy cost twice the precision on every delay, one more bit per pixel, and 4.7× less tolerance for lost light, while the one error that already fails against real hardware does not move at all. Its number is real and measured exactly like the headline, but what it asks of the manufacturing is something this project has no evidence anyone can deliver.

There is also something the budget cannot say. At 0.53 mm spacing a ±10 µm positioning error goes from 0.33% of the gap to 1.9% of it, and past roughly forty plates the stack is better described as a solid block of glass than as separate sheets with air between them. That is a different thing to manufacture, and the budget covers component errors only.

Fig 7 asks the other one, which is what the extra fifty-one plates actually bought, and it is drawn the same way as the front page’s matrix → so the two can be set side by side.

What depth fixes is the digits that were collapsing onto 3. On the 5-mask design a 5 is called a 3 forty-one times and an 8 is called a 3 thirty-seven times; those are its two worst cells by a wide margin. Deep, they fall to 6 and 11, and 5 goes from right 109 times out of 186 to 164. Nearly the whole ten-point gain is that one repair.

What it does not fix is 4 against 9. Mistaking a 4 for a 9 happens more often on the deep stack, 18 times against 15, and 7 called 9 barely moves, 14 against 12. What vanishes is the reverse direction: 9 read as 4 drops from 29 to 2. So the confusion stops being mutual without stopping. Fifty-six plates of steering separate the pairs that a coarser routing merged, and leave the pair that shares an upper loop in the same place roughly where it was. That is the shape of a better approximation of the same limited operation, which is the subject of the next section.

None of that says anything about fragility, and the figure is not evidence either way. For the record, at the 0.35 rad of delay error the 5-mask design merely degrades under, this model is at chance, predicting almost nothing but 5 and 6 for every input. The table above is where that lives.

Why more of the same runs out

Depth cannot fix what depth is not the problem with

The flattening curve is not bad luck, and it is not a training failure. It follows from what this machine is. The physics page shows → that crossing a gap is linear in the light and passing through a mask is linear in the light, so a stack of them, however tall, collapses into a single linear operation: one matrix, applied once. Adding masks adds adjustable numbers, and those numbers let the matrix land closer to whatever matrix you wanted, under the constraint that it be buildable out of delay-only plates separated by air. What they do not add is a second kind of step.

That is the whole difference from an ordinary neural network. There, depth compounds: each layer bends its input through a nonlinearity before the next layer sees it, and stacking them builds up functions no single layer could express. Here nothing bends between the plates. Fifty-six masks and five masks are both one linear map followed by one squaring at the detector. Adding plates buys a better approximation of the same limited thing. The curve flattens because it is converging on the best that thing can do.

So the answer is not more masks. It is a nonlinear step somewhere in the middle, which is exactly what this design does not have and, per its scope, does not try to build. The chip can be given one from its own parts, and the next page does →

How the field is trying to get one

Putting a nonlinearity into the light

This is an active research problem, and the approaches split by where the nonlinearity is made to come from. Three families, and a note on what this project can and cannot vouch for.

Turn some of the light into a current. Detect it part-way through and use the electronics, in one of two ways that cost different things. The first re-sends a fresh beam: detect, compute the nonlinearity in a circuit, and modulate new light for the next layer. It certainly works, since the detector’s squaring is already a nonlinearity, and working photonic processors run exactly this loop with the step between layers done digitally.2 What it costs is the thing optics was supposed to avoid: a conversion, and its energy and latency, at every layer. The second keeps the same light: tap a small fraction off to a photodiode, and let its amplified current set the delay of an interferometer that the rest of that light crosses.3 The signal stays optical and keeps its phase, and only the tapped fraction is converted, but every waveguide still needs its own detector, amplifier and delay line. A chip version exists.4

Use a material that responds to intensity. Saturable absorbers, which become more transparent as light gets brighter, and phase-change materials, which switch state. The optics stays optical. The difficulty is that these effects are weak at the power levels an efficient inference engine wants to run at, which is the tension the whole approach lives inside.

Change where the input enters. The most interesting of the three, because it sidesteps the problem rather than paying for it. Wanjura and Marquardt show that if the input is encoded not in the incoming wave but in the physical parameters that control how the wave scatters, the map from input to output is genuinely nonlinear even though the wave physics stays perfectly linear.1 The nonlinearity comes from the encoding, not from any material. They propose an integrated-photonics implementation and show the gradients needed for training can be measured directly in scattering experiments.

What this project can vouch for is narrow. The measurement here is that a stack of phase masks is one linear operator and that scaling it has sharply diminishing returns. Of the routes above it has tested exactly one, the same-light loop, on the chip rather than the stack, and only as far as the next page measures it →: it makes a second layer pay, and pays for it in light. Nothing here is evidence about the other routes.

Reading the numbers on this page honestly

Two protocols, and a confound worth ruling out

The sweep accuracies come from a deliberately short protocol, 20,000 images for 12 passes, because the question there is which arrangement wins rather than what final accuracy it would reach. They are not comparable to the 0.799 headline, which came from 60,000 images and 40 passes. The fair comparison for the 5-mask geometry under the short protocol is 0.771. The 56-mask model is the exception: it was trained at the full budget and scored on the held-back test images, so its 0.9040 and the headline 0.7990 are the same measurement.

There is a confound to rule out. Adding a mask adds 128² more delays, so more masks also means more adjustable numbers, and the sweep alone cannot separate “depth helps” from “more numbers help”. Two parameter-matched pairs settle it. 56 masks on a 128² grid and 14 on a 256² grid both carry 917,504 delays and score 0.889 against 0.856; the 80-versus-20 pair, both at 1,310,720, gives 0.891 against 0.870. At equal parameter count, depth wins. Each arrangement was run once, so trust the trend across the whole range rather than the gap between neighbours.

The sweep never touched the held-back test images, since that set is handed to the as-built model and every downstream number is quoted from it. A separate slice was carved out of the training data instead and used once, at the end, to score the arrangement that had already won.

What comes next

What a foundry actually achieves. The chip has since had the same treatment →, and the failure mode is confirmed: errors there accumulate in sequence, so it needs its delays ten times more accurate than the stack does. What is missing is the other half of the comparison. Every “realistic value” on the chip side is unsourced, because it needs a chip foundry’s process data rather than the display-device measurements this page rests on, and nothing here will invent one.

Single photons through the same mesh. Send light through the trained chip one photon at a time, with the photons indistinguishable, and the output statistics stop being classical. The odds of each pattern are then governed by a quantity called the permanent of the matrix, which is famously hard to compute, and that difficulty is the point of boson sampling. The planned work computes those odds and shows the Hong–Ou–Mandel dip, where two identical photons entering a 50:50 coupler always leave together. Only the light going in changes; the mesh is identical.

The measurement

Two sweeps: plate separation at a fixed mask count, then the same total reach spent different ways. Wrap-around error is measured against a reference run on a padded grid where it cannot occur, and decides which arrangements are worth training at all.

Reproducing it

apps/sweep_optics.py runs both sweeps and saves a checkpoint per arrangement; apps/sweep_report.py writes the figure and the data this page plots. Fixed random seeds throughout.

Status

Live work, not a result. The numbers here move as more arrangements finish. The 5-mask design and everything downstream of it stay exactly where they are.
