One bend in the light

A second layer, paid for in light

Two interferometer meshes with nothing between them compute exactly what one mesh computes. This page puts one physical nonlinearity between them, an interferometer that sets its own delay from the light passing through it, and asks the project’s question of the result: what does crossing the wall buy on this chip, and what does it cost to build? It buys a second layer that is worth something. It costs light, not precision.

The wall, on this chip

Two meshes with nothing between them are one mesh

One layer of the chip is a mesh of Mach–Zehnder interferometers that realises a matrix, U·Σ·V: two lossless meshes around a row of attenuators. Stack a second layer after the first with nothing in between, and the two matrices simply multiply:

(U2Σ2V2)(U1Σ1V1)=M

That product is one more matrix of the same size, which a single layer could already have realised. So a second linear layer adds adjustable numbers and no new kind of function, and the measurement agrees exactly. Trained three times each under one protocol, one layer scores 0.8598 and two layers score 0.8604. Twice the hardware buys six ten-thousandths, which is nothing.

Those numbers come from a different input than the rest of the site. The chip on the earlier pages reads a 6×6 shrunken digit, 36 brightnesses on 36 waveguides. Here the digit enters as its 16 lowest spatial frequencies, each a complex number carrying both a brightness and a phase, which is what a lens does to an image for free. That is an encoding change, not a task change: still the same handwritten digits, still one digit per input. Why the input had to change is the subject of the gate below; the short version is that on the 6×6 input nothing this page tried made depth pay.

The only nonlinear step in either layer is the detector at the very end, which squares the light. To make the second layer count, something has to bend the light between the meshes.

The device

An interferometer that sets its own delay from its own light

The device is Williamson and co-workers’ electro-optic activation.1 On each waveguide, a tap (a coupler that splits off a small fixed fraction α of the light, here a tenth) sends that sample to a photodiode, which turns light into an electric current in proportion to its power. The ratio is the photodiode’s responsivity, about one amp per watt. An amplifier turns the current into a voltage, and that voltage drives the delay inside one more Mach–Zehnder interferometer, which the other nine tenths of the same light then crosses. A length of waveguide holds that light back while the electronics catch up.

Nothing is converted and re-sent. The signal never leaves the waveguide, so it stays coherent: it keeps the phase relationships the next mesh depends on, and no digital step sits between the layers. What comes out is the input times a factor that depends on the input’s own power:

f(z)=j1−αexp(−jh)cos(h)z,h=gφ|z|2+φb2

Here h is half the delay on the device’s interferometer: a fixed bias φb set by a steady voltage, plus whatever the light itself writes through the photodiode. How many radians each watt writes is set entirely by electronics:

gφ=παGℜVπ

with G the amplifier’s gain in ohms, ℜ the responsivity, and Vπ the half-wave voltage, the voltage that delays the light by half a wavelength. The fraction of the light that gets through is then

T(P)=(1−α)cos2(gφP+φb2)

This is not a new kind of component. It is the same interferometer the meshes are built from, with a delay the light sets for itself, and the equation above equals one built from this project’s own coupler and phase-shifter matrices to 2.6×10−14, round-off, over two thousand random cases.

The widget draws that transmission. The bias decides whether weak light gets through, and the gain decides where the device switches. With the bias at π, the setting the paper and this chip both use, the device is dark for weak light and opens as the light brightens, a shape often compared to a ReLU. Hold that comparison loosely until the next section.

Live. The device as trained, in watts: tap 0.1, bias π, gain 157 rad/W. Move the sliders to see what each control does to the curve; the trained chip below keeps its own settings fixed.

Where the numbers come from. The tap, bias and gain are the paper’s design values, the gain expressed per watt at a 1 mW input. The responsivity is measured: a germanium photodiode on a commercial silicon platform reaches about 1.0 A/W, read off a published plot.3 The 10 V half-wave voltage is the paper’s worked example, and the 5 kΩ amplifier gain is whatever makes the product come out; no datasheet in this project shows that gain at the 10 GHz the device is designed to run at, and the source files mark it unsourced.

The gate

Does it make depth real?

Before this device was built into anything, the paper’s own claim had to be reproduced here, with a rule written down in advance: on the paper’s setup the activated pair of meshes must beat the plain pair by at least 5 points, and on this project’s own input it must beat a single mesh by more than run-to-run noise. A first quick attempt did not reproduce the gain, so the possible causes were tried one at a time, each at three random starts.

The paper’s gain reproduces. On its own setup the device is worth 8.8 points over two plain meshes, against the 7.2 the paper reports. On the 6×6 input it is not worth anything measurable, and neither the paper’s style of mesh nor letting training set the device’s gain and bias changed that. So, as the rule allowed, the chip on this page reads the paper’s input, and with this project’s ordinary readout the device makes a second layer worth 3.4 points. Hold the attenuators ahead of the device to what a passive chip can do, so nothing amplifies the light on its way in, and it is still worth 3.3.

One run of the gate failed for a reason worth recording. Every activated network trained to chance, because the standard function for building a complex number from a size and an angle computes its slope with the wrong sign when the size is negative, and at this bias it always is. The forward calculation was right to thirteen decimal places; only the slope was wrong. A test now checks that slope against finite differences.

Now look at where the trained device actually operates. Pick a digit and watch its sixteen modes land on the curve.

Live. The trained two-layer chip, 1,056 settings stored at 16 bits, running here one digit at a time; it agrees with the reference model on every gallery digit. Each dot is one mode arriving at its device. The slider sets the light entering the chip, with every source of noise switched off.

No mode ever gets near the threshold. Across all 10,000 test digits, none of the 160,000 mode arrivals passes half-open. The light writes a median of 3.9 thousandths of a radian on each device, and the bank lets through 2.5×10−5 of what reaches it. The device is not working as a switch here. It is working as a cube: deep in its dark region the output is minus a constant times |z|2z, the input cubed, and that is the whole of the nonlinearity that makes the second layer pay.

A cube has a property that matters for everything below. Scale the input up or down and the output scales by the cube, but every mode by the same factor, and the readout divides by the total anyway. So with no noise the chip computes the same answer from a femtowatt to 30 mW, more than thirteen decades: the trained function does not care how bright the light is. What changes is how much light reaches the detectors, which falls as the square of the input power on top of the input power itself. Drag the slider down and watch the photon count.

What it costs

The same budget, on the activated chip

The as-built model was rebuilt in MATLAB, reproduced the trained chip’s 0.8864 exactly on all 10,000 test digits, and was then broken one imperfection at a time, as the earlier budgets were: each error drawn ten times at random, each chip judged against 95% of its own ideal, and each limit quoted as the largest error that holds and the smallest that fails. The one-layer chip on the same input, at 0.8555, went through the very same sweeps, so every row below is one protocol and two machines.

The chip does not have to be built any more precisely. Delays, couplers and heaters keep the one-layer chip’s limits to the same step of the sweep. What the device adds is a demand for light the linear chip never had. One mesh works on anything above 10 µW at its design speed. The activated chip needs 10 mW, a thousand times more, because its readout gets light as the cube of the input. And it has a ceiling: above 100 mW the brightest modes write whole radians on their devices, the cube stops being a cube, and the chip computes something it was never trained to. At 300 mW it is down to 0.35. The linear chip has no ceiling at all.

Fig 1 also shows something about speed. The linear chip only cares about the energy per input, power times time, so its two curves are the same curve shifted. The activated chip responds to power, so short bright pulses beat long dim ones: at one input per 100 ps it needs about 1 pJ of light per input, at one per millisecond about 100 nJ, a hundred thousand times more energy for the same answer. The device wants to run fast, which is what it was designed for. At the setting the paper chose, 1 mW at 10 GHz, it gets about one photon per input at the detectors, and classifies at 0.18.

At 1 mW these are the tightest numbers anywhere in this project: a bias held to a thousandth of a radian, thirty times tighter than the mesh’s own delays, and couplers within three parts in ten thousand of an even split. All three bad ones share one mechanism. The device sits on its dark setting, and each of these errors leaks a little unbent light through it, competing with a cubed signal that is itself only a thousandth of the field. The cubed signal grows with power and the leak does not, so at 30 mW every one of them relaxes thirty times or more, as Fig 2 shows. Wavelength drift is the same story in disguise: a longer wavelength lowers the device’s bias a little, which is why the activated chip holds 2 nm at 1 mW and 10 nm at 30.

Gain calibration is free. A wrong gain on every device multiplies every cube by the same factor, and the readout divides that out. It is the one error a builder could correct afterwards, and the one that does not matter.

Where precision goes. Fig 3 splits the delay error by layer. Errors in the first mesh fail at 0.03 rad; errors in the second hold to 0.05, looser than a single mesh. An error ahead of the device is cubed along with the light, tripling it; one after the device is not. A builder spends the precision budget on the first mesh and can relax the second.

Eight predictions, and what came back

The first miss is the useful one. On the earlier pages the chip needed its delays ten times more accurate than the glass stack because errors pile up through 72 interferometers in a row. Doubling the row did not tighten the limit here at all, because what sets it is which side of the device an error sits on, not how many interferometers it crosses. Serial pile-up is real in one linear mesh; across a nonlinearity it is not the whole story.

The cone of interferometers that cannot affect the answer, worked out on paper before anything was measured, survives exactly. Six of the second layer’s last mesh are outside it, the same six the closed form names. The first layer has none, because the device hands every mode on to a full second mesh.

Finding · crossing the wall costs light, not precision

One interferometer per waveguide, setting its own delay from its own light, makes a second layer worth 3.4 points where a second plain layer is worth nothing. It does so as a pure cube, so the chip needs 1,000× the light of one mesh at its design speed and stops working above 100 mW. Its own tolerances are the tightest in the study at low power and relax as the light grows, so the place to run it is the top of that window. Nothing has to be built more precisely; it has to be run brighter.

The device

Williamson et al.’s activation,1 built in the code from this project’s own interferometer matrices and held to the paper’s closed form to round-off. A chip version exists,2 trained on the hardware itself, which this project’s train-then-build premise rules out; it is evidence that the device can be made, not a method copied here.

Reproducing it

apps/eo_gate.py runs the gate; apps/train_deep_mesh.py trains both chips; run_error_budget_deep.m runs the budget in about two minutes, seeds 29000–50999. The full record, predictions included, is docs/phase5_activation.md.
