Free space and silicon, side by side

The same machine, built two ways

A stack of etched glass and a silicon chip threaded with light-carrying channels look like unrelated devices. Strip both to their skeletons and the same sequence appears: a layer of delays you train, then a layer of fixed hardware that blends the channels together, repeated, and closed by a detector that measures brightness. They differ on exactly one number, and that number sets everything else.

The other optical computer

An interferometer is two splitters around an adjustable delay

On a chip, light does not spread through open air. It runs along waveguides, narrow channels of silicon that trap light the way a pipe carries water. Each channel carries one stream, called a mode, and streams in separate channels cannot mix unless the hardware deliberately brings them together. The component that does that is a directional coupler: run two channels close enough and light leaks between them, and at the right length exactly half crosses over.

A Mach–Zehnder interferometer is not exotic hardware. It is two of those 50:50 couplers with an adjustable delay θ in between and another delay φ in front, which written out is B·P(θ)·B·P(φ). Split the light, hold one arm back, recombine: how much the two halves reinforce or cancel decides how much leaves by each output. That combination is unitary for any setting of the two delays, meaning it only ever redistributes light between the outputs and never creates or destroys any, which is exactly what passive hardware can do. Tile these across neighbouring pairs of channels and you get a Clements rectangle of n(n−1)/2 of them.

Such a mesh can produce any unitary operation at all, and the proof is a recipe rather than an existence argument. You take the target operation and use one interferometer at a time to zero out its entries one by one, until nothing is left but the identity; run the recipe backwards and you have the settings. Attacking it from one side only gives the triangular Reck mesh; attacking from both sides gives the balanced Clements rectangle, which is half as deep and loses less light. Put a row of simple brightness adjustments between two such meshes and you can build any real matrix, not just a unitary one, using the singular-value decomposition U·Σ·V†, the standard factorisation that splits any matrix into a rotation, a stretch along each axis, and another rotation. Both recipes rebuild randomly drawn target operations to ~10−15, which is this side’s correctness anchor.

Trained on the same digits it scores 0.736, just shy of the glass stack’s 0.799, but with 2,628 adjustable numbers against 81,920, about 31× fewer, and able to implement any linear map at all rather than only the ones a stack of masks can reach. The gap is not a modelling failure. The chip’s area grows as the square of the number of channels, so feeding it a large image is prohibitive, and MNIST has to be shrunk to 6×6 = 36 channels first. It starves on how much input it can take in, not on what it can compute. That trade between chip area and input size is the central structural difference between the two processors.

One finding falls out of the physics rather than the training. A mesh with no power source cannot amplify, since at best it passes all the light through and in practice loses some, so a buildable Σ needs every one of its stretch factors to be 1 or less. Several of the trained ones come out above 1. A real device would need an amplifier, or would have to scale everything down and pay for it in lost light.

The correspondence

Why they are the same machine

A chip of waveguides looks nothing like a stack of etched glass, and everything above reads as two unrelated devices. They are not. Strip both to their skeletons and the same sequence appears: a layer of delays you train, then a layer of fixed hardware that blends the channels, repeated, and closed by a detector that measures brightness. A phase mask is a column of delays. A 3 mm air gap is a column of couplers. In both machines the only thing training ever adjusts is a delay, and in both, the blending step is fixed by the hardware and cannot be programmed.

This is a shared abstraction, not shared hardware. 16,384 pixels and 36 modes are nowhere near the same scale, and no rearrangement turns one into the other. What transfers is the skeleton, not the device.

They part on exactly one thing, how far a single blending layer carries information sideways, and that one number sets the depth, the size and the way each machine fails. Diffraction hands you a wide reach for nothing, but you cannot choose it: the 12.5 px per gap is fixed by the distance, the wavelength and the pixel size, and it is the same everywhere on the plate. A coupler reaches exactly one neighbour, so you need as many columns as you have channels, and in exchange every one of those couplings can be set individually, which is what lets the mesh reach any operation at all. Going from free space to a chip trades one wide, fixed, unsteerable blending step for many narrow ones you can steer. The rest is packaging.

Finding · connectivity is a bound the geometry has to clear

Six gaps of 3 mm let each pixel’s light spread 74.8 px sideways in total. The hardest case the design has to cover is a pixel at one edge of the entrance influencing the detector pixel farthest from it, and that distance is 74 px. Every input pixel can therefore reach every detector, which is the precondition for the network being able to compute anything at all. Push the plates closer than 2.967 mm apart and it stops being true: parts of the input become physically invisible to parts of the readout, whatever the masks are set to. The 3 mm separation was chosen to sit above that bound, which is why it does. Training never sees this constraint and could not have satisfied it; it is settled by the geometry before any mask is fitted. The mesh arrives at the same guarantee from the other direction, since 36 columns for 36 channels is exactly the bound Clements proves you need, so there full connectivity follows from the layout rather than from a spacing choice.

A limit on what can reach what is not a claim about how much light actually does. The very edge of that cone is the steepest ray the grid can represent, and it carries little energy in practice. What the bound tells you is where the design sits relative to a hard wall, and the answer is “just inside it, by accident”.

The two machines also share their ceiling. Everything the physics page argues about linearity → applies to the mesh unchanged: one linear optical step, then a detector that squares. Swapping free space for silicon buys steerability, a smaller footprint and something you can actually manufacture. It buys no extra computational power at all.

How they fail is where they differ, and that is the part this project is built to measure. On the chip, small errors pile up as the light passes through 72 interferometers one after another, and couplers that split slightly unevenly or lose a little light make the whole operation leak. In the glass stack the errors act side by side instead, one per pixel, and what dominates is whether each pixel’s delay stays sharply confined to that pixel. Error budget · the chip

What breaks it

Both machines have now been handed to a separate model of themselves as they would really be built and broken on purpose, one imperfection at a time. The two answers are not the same answer, and the difference is the point of building both.

0.03 radis how accurately a delay on the chip must be set. The glass stack tolerates ten times more
±0.01is how evenly a coupler must split. A phase mask has no such part, so this constraint is new

The reason is depth, in series. In the glass stack the errors act side by side: five masks, one delay per pixel, each pixel wrong on its own and the mistakes averaging out across the plate. On the chip the light passes through 72 columns of interferometers one after another, and every column hands the previous column’s error forward along with the signal. Nothing averages. A splitter that sends 51% one way instead of 50% is a trivial error in one component and a visible one by the far end.

That is what the panel below shows. Each square is one entry of the operation the chip performs — how much of one input channel arrives at one output channel — drawn for the trained chip, then again with every coupler mis-made by a random amount, then as the difference between them. The damage does not stay near the couplers that were made wrong. It fills in everywhere.

Finding · a quarter of the chip does not have to be built well

Detuning each of the 1,260 interferometers in turn, one at a time, and measuring how far the output moves gives a map of which parts of the device matter. 156 of the 630 interferometers in the second mesh move the output by exactly nothing — not a little, not below the noise, but zero. They sit outside the readout’s reach: only the first ten channels are ever measured, a coupler in column c can influence a channel at most 36−c+1 steps away, and those 156 are further than that from anything the detector sees. Whatever they are set to, and however badly they are made, the answer is the same. A quarter of that mesh needs no fabrication tolerance at all, which is a property of the wiring rather than of the training, and it is the blank triangle filling the lower right of the panel below.

One thing this page does not tell you: whether a real foundry can hold ±0.01 on a coupler. The limits above are measured properties of this network and its wiring, and they will not move. What a fabricated part actually achieves is a different kind of number, and it has to come from a chip foundry’s published process data — not from the display-device measurements the glass stack’s budget rests on. That sourcing has not been done, so every “realistic value” column in the chip’s tolerance document reads UNSOURCED, and there is no pass/fail verdict anywhere in it. Publishing the limits first and the comparison against reality second is a deliberate order, not an oversight: the limits are finished work, and inventing the other column to complete the table would be the only dishonest thing available here.

Derived, never typed

Every number in the table above is exported from the trained models rather than chosen. A test re-derives each one from the physics and the model code, so how far light spreads, the detector layout and the mesh tiling all fail loudly if the design drifts away from them.

Verified

That an interferometer conserves light at every setting; that both recipes rebuild a target operation to ~10−15; that the arbitrary-real-matrix layer works; and that the PyTorch mesh matches the plain NumPy one.

Still open

What a fabricated part actually achieves. The limits above are measured and finished; the column beside them that says how close a real process gets is empty, and stays empty until it can be filled from a chip foundry’s published data rather than the display-device literature the glass stack’s budget rests on. A second mesh size would also turn the depth argument into a trend rather than a single point.
