How the light is moved, and where the method stops working

Everything rests on moving light from one plane to the next

Before anything can be trained, the simulation has to move light across 3 mm of air exactly, and it has to know the point at which it can no longer do that. One method does the moving, one criterion marks that point, and one structural fact caps what the whole idea can ever compute.

The propagator

Decompose into plane waves, delay each one, add them back up

Any pattern of light, however complicated, can be written as a sum of plane waves: perfectly flat, evenly spaced wavefronts, each travelling in one particular direction. That is useful because a plane wave crossing a gap does something trivial. It stays a plane wave and simply falls behind by an amount set by its direction. So the recipe is to split the light into plane waves with a Fourier transform, hold each one back by its own amount, and add them all back together. That is the whole angular-spectrum method: transform, multiply by

H=exp(i2πz1λ2−f2)

and transform back. It makes no small-angle approximation, the simplification most textbook diffraction formulas lean on and the one usually called paraxial, so it stays exact even for steeply travelling light. That is why Fresnel, Fraunhofer and the differentiable PyTorch layer are all checked against it rather than the other way round. Against a Gaussian beam with a known closed-form answer it agrees to ~2×10−8.

Two behaviours fall straight out of that square root. Where f2>1λ2 the quantity under it goes negative, which corresponds to light angled so steeply it cannot actually travel. Computing the root over the complex numbers makes those components die away with distance instead, which is the correct physics: these are evanescent waves, which cling to the surface rather than propagating. No special case is needed. And over a long enough distance the multiplier H starts spinning faster than the grid has points to follow it. A grid that cannot keep up with a wave reports a slower one that happens to match at the sample points, which is aliasing, and the answer silently becomes wrong. Setting H to zero past the point where the grid can still track it keeps the method honest at long range, at the cost of throwing away the steepest light.

The edge is a single criterion, zcrit=N·dx2/λ, which is 15.4 mm here against 3 mm gaps. It is checked every time the code runs, not only in the tests. Note what it does not contain. It depends on the wavelength, the pixel size and the number of pixels, but not on the width of the opening the light passes through, which is why the aperture control below does not move the sampling threshold no matter how far you drag it.

Below is that calculation, running. It is worth knowing what you are looking at before you start dragging, because the picture is less obvious than it appears.

Light enters through a hole, the aperture, and you are shown what it looks like after travelling a distance z. Aperture shape decides the pattern’s symmetry: a round hole gives concentric rings, a square one gives a cross, because the edges are what bend the light and their orientation survives all the way to the far end. Aperture width works backwards from intuition, and this is the single most surprising control here: narrowing the hole makes the pattern wider. Squeezing a wave in space forces it to spread in angle. Distance and wavelength both scale that spreading up, so a longer wavelength or a longer flight both blur the pattern outward.

Grid N is the only control that is not physics. It is how many samples the simulation uses across the window, and it changes nothing about the light. What it changes is whether the computer can still faithfully represent that light. Coarse grids cannot hold steeply angled waves, which is what the amber flag is about: past z > z_crit the method starts throwing away the steepest content rather than failing outright, so it is flagged instead of trusted. Raise N and the flag clears, because a finer grid can carry more angle.

Two things to actually try. Drag aperture width to its smallest and watch the rings swell to fill the window. Then push distance to its maximum and watch the flag turn amber, which is the moment the simulation stops being trustworthy and says so.

Live. The diffraction is computed in your browser, the same physics as the Python original and checked against it to better than 10−6. Move any control; the sampling flag turns amber the moment z > z_crit.

Drawing it without lying about it

Why the 3D stack has haze in it and no rays

The light drawn between the mask planes on the front page is not decoration. One 3 mm gap can be cut into several shorter steps and re-run, because propagating a distance and then another gives exactly the same answer as propagating the sum in one go: H(z1)·H(z2)=H(z1+z2). The one thing that would break that equality is the band limit described above, and below zcrit it is switched off entirely. At 3 mm against 15.4 mm it is switched off, so those in-between planes are the real light, not an interpolation between the planes on either side. Above zcrit the equality genuinely does break, and the test suite asserts both halves of that.

No rays are drawn, deliberately. Light here is treated as a wave, not as travelling arrows, and drawing straight lines from digit to detector would misrepresent the one thing the figure exists to show: that every point of the input reaches every detector at once. The stack is 18 mm long across an opening of 1.02 mm, about 18:1, so drawn true to scale it is an unreadable needle. The depth is therefore squashed, and the figure prints its own squash factor on its face rather than quietly flattering the geometry.

The drawing also cannot touch the answer. The prediction still comes from the canonical six propagations, and a test asserts the ten class scores are bit-for-bit identical whether the extra in-between planes are computed or not.

The limit, stated precisely

Optical depth is not depth in the machine-learning sense

Crossing a gap is linear in the light. Passing through a mask is linear in the light. Doing one after the other is therefore still linear, and the whole stack collapses into a single multiplication by one big matrix:

Eout=M·Ein
M=PLDLPL−1DL−1…D1P0

Each P is one gap of air the light crosses, and each D is one mask it passes through.

This matters more than it sounds. No matter how many masks and gaps are stacked up, the map from input light to output light is one matrix. Extra layers add adjustable numbers, and they let M come closer to whatever matrix you wanted, subject to the physical constraint that it be buildable out of delay-only masks separated by air. What they do not add is any of the compounding power that stacking layers gives an ordinary neural network, where each layer bends the result before the next one sees it. Depth here is not depth in that sense at all.

The single exception is the detection. The score for class c is the brightness added up over its detector region, and brightness is the square of the wave’s height. Work that through and the score comes out as

sc=Ein†AcEin

with

Ac=M†RcM≻0

In words: each score is a quadratic form, meaning the inputs enter multiplied together in pairs rather than one at a time, and the ≻0 says the resulting score can never go negative. The classifier computes one of these per class and picks the largest. That is strictly more powerful than a plain linear classifier, and it is exactly one nonlinear step: linear optics, square the magnitude, add up.

One consequence is worth pulling out, because it is the only other place anything nonlinear touches the raw pixels: how the image is written into the beam. Encoding it as delays rather than brightness passes the pixel values through exp(i·π·image) before the optics ever sees them, and that step is not linear. This is why the choice of encoding measurably changes the accuracy that can be reached. It is quietly doing work the linear optics downstream cannot do.

By the project’s own scope this limit is measured and described, not engineered around. There is no attempt to add a nonlinear step in the optics, no exotic materials, and no larger electronic network bolted on the end to compensate. If a bigger electronic stage would lift the accuracy, that is a result worth reporting, not a bug to fix.

One thing this argument does not settle is how close any particular stack gets to the best operator its masks could implement. Five masks score 0.799, and it would be easy to read that as the ceiling above being reached. It is not: stacking far more of them keeps paying, and that is a page of its own →

Checked against known answers

Every case here has a textbook solution to check against. The angular spectrum matches a Gaussian beam to ~2×10−8; Fresnel matches it inside its valid range to ~9×10−9; a round hole gives the classic bullseye pattern with its first dark ring exactly where theory puts it.

Enforced, not assumed

The sampling limit is checked whenever the code runs, not only in the tests: check_sampling returns a report, and the explorer flags the crossing live. Past zcrit the method does not crash or obviously misbehave, which is precisely why it has to be flagged.

Scope

Light is treated as a single wave with no direction of vibration, which rules out polarisation effects, and there is no full electromagnetic solver anywhere. If real component data is ever wanted it gets imported as measurements rather than simulated here.
