The question the project exists to answer

How precisely must a photonic chip be built before it stops computing what it was trained to compute?

Everything so far was ideal. The masks were exact, the plates sat exactly 3 mm apart, and every pixel delayed the light by exactly what it was trained to. Now those same trained numbers are handed to a separate model of the machine as it would really be built, and broken on purpose, one imperfection at a time, until the number that decides whether this is buildable falls out.

0.25 pxis how far one pixel’s delay may bleed into its neighbours before accuracy goes
≈1 pxis how far it bleeds on a real display chip. That is four times too much

Method

Now break it on purpose

The trained numbers cross a one-way handover: a single file carrying the phase masks, the layout, the operating conditions and the test images, passed to a completely separate MATLAB model of the machine as it would really be built, the as-built model, which never writes anything back. That one-way boundary is deliberate. It keeps the ideal network and the imperfect one from quietly contaminating each other. Nothing is retrained on the far side, and no error model exists on the near side.

That second model then re-scores the very same network under each manufacturing imperfection in turn: delays set slightly wrong, delays available only in coarse steps, light lost along the way, the laser drifting off its colour, heat from one pixel leaking into the next, and noise in the detectors. Each one is run many times over with different random draws, which is what a Monte Carlo sweep means, with the random seeds recorded so any run can be reproduced exactly, and every error size for this machine traced to a published measurement cited in the code at the point it is used. The chip, at the foot of this page, has been through the identical procedure — and is explicit about the one thing it cannot yet source.

One control turns this from a demonstration into a measurement. With no error injected at all, the second model reproduces 0.7990 exactly, the same number the PyTorch model gets on the same 2,000 images. Two independently written implementations agreeing to the last digit means any drop below it is caused by the injected error and nothing else. That anchor has held through every change in the project.

The pass mark throughout is 95% of the ideal accuracy, which works out to ≥0.7591. Limits are quoted as the bracket the sweep actually resolves, holds at X, fails at Y, rather than interpolating a crossing point between two tested values, so they stay comparable from one model to the next.

Binding constraint

Crosstalk between neighbouring pixels sets the tolerance, and it is the one that fails. Setting one pixel’s delay disturbs the pixels around it, because heat spreads and electric fields spill sideways, so a pattern meant to be sharp comes out blurred. Accuracy holds while that blur stays at 0.25 px and fails by 0.5 px; three quarters of a pixel of blur collapses the network to little better than guessing. On a standard LCoS device, the liquid-crystal display chip these masks would be written to, the blur is about 1 px, so this design as specified would not work on one. The ranking is unambiguous: crosstalk is far worse than delay error, which beats detector power and lost light, which in turn are far worse than wavelength drift and coarse delay steps. To stay above ≥0.7591, the design needs delays accurate to 0.3 rad, a twenty-first of a wavelength (λ/21), at least 3 bits of control per pixel, laser drift under 10 nm, and at least 1 pW of light over 1 ms. What decides whether this is buildable is not the precision of each delay or how finely it can be set. It is how sharply each pixel’s delay can be kept away from its neighbours.

Source 1 of 6 · the one that fails

Crosstalk: pixels will not stay out of each other

A phase mask is only a mask because neighbouring pixels can hold different delays. Set one pixel and its neighbours shift too, because heat spreads through the substrate and the electric field that addresses one cell spills into the next. The pattern the design wants sharp comes out smeared, and the mask stops being able to express what training asked of it.

Drag the bleed and watch the close-up. Accuracy holds at 0.25 px and fails by 0.5 px; three quarters of a pixel leaves the network little better than guessing. A standard LCoS device, the liquid-crystal display chip these masks would be written to, bleeds about 1 px. That is four times more than the design survives, and it is why this one source decides the whole question.

Source 2 of 6

Delay error: every pixel a little bit wrong

Nothing sets a phase exactly. Etch depth varies across a plate, and a modulator addressed by a voltage lands near the value it was asked for rather than on it. The error is independent from pixel to pixel, which makes it scattered rather than structured, and that turns out to matter: the large-scale pattern the masks rely on survives a surprising amount of speckle.

Accuracy holds to 0.3 rad, a twenty-first of a wavelength (λ/21), and fails by 0.5. A well-calibrated device sits near λ/100, about 0.05 rad, which is an order of magnitude inside the requirement. This source is comfortable.

Source 3 of 6

Detector noise: light arrives in countable lumps

The readout is ten sums of brightness, and brightness is a number of photons. Dim the beam far enough and those counts get noisy in a way no amount of care in the optics can fix, since it comes from the light itself. On top of that the sensor adds its own read noise, and the converter rounds whatever survives to a fixed number of steps.

Accuracy holds down to 1 pW over a 1 ms exposure and fails at 0.1 pW. The design’s nominal operating point is 1 mW, which is nine orders of magnitude brighter than the edge. There is an enormous amount of room here.

Source 4 of 6 · the subtle one

Lost light: no effect at all, until suddenly it is fatal

Every surface reflects a little and every millimetre of glass absorbs a little. The intuition is that this must degrade the answer, and the intuition is wrong. The readout compares the ten detector boxes against each other, so dimming all of them by the same factor leaves the ranking untouched. Uniform loss cancels exactly.

It bites only through the source above it. Lost light means fewer photons, and few enough photons means the noise in the previous section. That coupling is why loss cannot be swept on its own: measured at the nominal 1 mW it would read as no effect at any magnitude, which is true and useless. It is swept instead at the edge of the photon budget, where every 3 dB lost halves what arrives, and there it holds to 1 dB per mask.

Source 5 of 6

Wavelength drift: the colour the design assumed

The whole machine is built around one colour of light, 532 nm green. Let the laser drift and two things go wrong at once. The plates are a fixed depth of glass, so the delay they impose is no longer the delay that was trained. And diffraction is itself colour-dependent, so the light spreads a different distance in each gap and lands somewhere slightly different.

Accuracy holds to 10 nm of drift and fails by 20. Any laser with its temperature controlled holds far tighter than that, so this source is a non-issue in practice.

Source 6 of 6

Coarse control: only so many settings per pixel

The design asks each pixel for a phase anywhere in a continuous range. Real hardware offers a fixed number of steps, set by the resolution of whatever drives it. The continuum collapses onto a comb, and every pixel moves to the nearest tooth.

This is the most forgiving source in the budget. Accuracy holds at 3 bits, which is eight settings per pixel, and fails only at 2. Real devices offer 8 bits, or 256 settings. The design has five bits of margin it does not need.

What the six add up to

One source decides it, and it is not a close call

Five of the six are comfortable, several by orders of magnitude. Delay accuracy has a factor of ten in hand, bit depth has five bits, detection has nine decades, wavelength is a non-issue for any controlled source, and lost light does not act on its own at all. If crosstalk did not exist, this design would be buildable today with ordinary components.

Crosstalk does exist, and it fails by a factor of four. Everything else in this budget is a detail beside that. It is worth being precise about what that does and does not mean: it is a statement about this design on today’s modulators, not a proof that free-space optical inference cannot be built. Bigger pixels, a different addressing scheme or a fabricated plate rather than a programmable one would all move it. What the number does settle is that the binding constraint is a hardware problem rather than a training one, and no amount of retraining touches it.

There is also something this budget does not cover at all. Every source here is a device error, something that goes wrong inside a component. None of them is a geometry error: plates a few microns out of position, rotated a fraction of a degree, or registered slightly off against each other. Nobody assembles a five-plane optical stack with the planes exactly 3.000 mm apart. For a free-space build that is very plausibly the harder problem, and this study has nothing measured to say about it.

The same treatment, on the chip

The other machine fails a different way

Everything above is the stack of glass. The interferometer mesh → has now been through the same procedure — its own trained settings, handed to its own as-built model, broken one imperfection at a time against its own 95% bar. Running it twice is what turns “these two machines fail differently” from a plausible sentence into a measurement, and the two answers are not the same answer.

0.03 radis how accurately a delay on the chip must be set, against the 0.3 rad the glass stack tolerates
±0.01is how evenly a coupler must split. A phase mask has no such part, so this constraint is new

Ten times more accurate, and three more bits of control per setting. The reason is depth, in series. In the glass stack the errors act side by side: five masks, one delay per pixel, each pixel wrong on its own and the mistakes averaging out across the plate. On the chip the light passes through 72 columns of interferometers one after another, and every column hands the previous column’s error forward along with the signal. Nothing averages. A splitter that sends 51% one way instead of 50% is a trivial error in one component and a visible one by the far end.

That is what the panel below shows. Each square is one entry of the operation the chip performs — how much of one input channel arrives at one output channel — drawn for the trained chip, then again with every coupler mis-made by a random amount, then as the difference between them. The damage does not stay near the couplers that were made wrong. It fills in everywhere.

Not everything moves. Detector noise lands on exactly the same limit, 1 pW at 1 ms, on a different architecture, a different test set and a different wavelength — as it should, since that is a statement about counting photons at the readout rather than about the optics in front of it. Getting the same number twice is a check on both budgets. Lost light is the one that changes character rather than magnitude: in the stack it is nearly uniform and cancels in the power-normalised readout, so it only bites through the photon budget, while on the chip the path length depends on which channel you are, so it stops cancelling and becomes a first-order source.

Finding · a quarter of the chip does not have to be built well

Detuning each of the 1,260 interferometers in turn, one at a time, and measuring how far the output moves gives a map of which parts of the device matter. 156 of the 630 interferometers in the second mesh move the output by exactly nothing — not a little, not below the noise, but zero. They sit outside the readout’s reach: only the first ten channels are ever measured, a coupler in column c can influence a channel at most 36−c+1 steps away, and those 156 are further than that from anything the detector sees. Whatever they are set to, and however badly they are made, the answer is the same. A quarter of that mesh needs no fabrication tolerance at all, which is a property of the wiring rather than of the training, and it is the blank triangle filling the lower right of the panel below. Tolerance is normally quoted per device and applied everywhere; here the boundary is known in closed form before anything is fabricated.

One thing this section does not tell you, and the difference matters. Every magnitude in the glass stack’s budget above traces to a published measurement, which is what lets it end in a verdict: crosstalk fails by a factor of four. The chip has no such column yet. Its limits are measured properties of the network and its wiring and will not move, but what a fabricated part actually achieves has to come from a chip foundry’s published process data — not the display-device literature everything above rests on. That sourcing has not been done, so every “realistic value” in the chip’s tolerance document reads UNSOURCED, and there is deliberately no pass/fail verdict anywhere in it. Publishing the limits first and the comparison against reality second is an order of work, not an oversight: the limits are finished, and inventing the other column to complete the table would be the only dishonest thing available here.

The boundary

Python designs the ideal network. A single file crosses to MATLAB, which models the device as it would really be built and never writes back. The separation between design and reality is enforced by that one-way handover rather than by discipline.

Every magnitude sourced, or flagged

For the glass stack, delay error, bit depth, drift, detector noise, lost light and pixel crosstalk each trace to a published measurement or a datasheet, cited where the number is defined. For the chip none of them does yet, and rather than invent one the study publishes the limits and marks the realistic column unsourced. Modelling choices are labelled as such on both sides.

Reproducible

The same seeds give the same answers, and the two studies draw from ranges that cannot collide. Each error-free baseline must come out at its model’s exact ideal — 0.7990 for the stack, 0.7355 for the chip — or that pair of models has drifted apart. A new model can be scored without disturbing either.
